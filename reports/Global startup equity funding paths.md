# Raise equity on the rails that fit

In 2025-2026, founders who raise outside investment mostly follow one ladder. Angels or a syndicate come first, often alongside an accelerator. Next comes a pre-seed round on a post-money SAFE, which is now about 90% of US pre-seed deals. A priced seed round of roughly $3-4M follows, selling about 18-20% of the company. Each step depends on warm introductions, because only about 10% of VC deals come from founders approaching cold. The money is real, but very unevenly spread. Global VC reached **US$728.3B in the first nine months of 2026**, yet AI took about **64% of Q3 dollars**, and seed deal counts keep falling. For a non-AI founder the median market is tougher than the headlines suggest. VC is also a rare path in general: only about **0.5% of entrepreneurs ever use it**, so before chasing it you should check honestly whether your business can return a venture fund. For the many businesses that cannot, there are equity and equity-like routes that still bring in investors without bank or high-interest debt. These include Reg CF or UK equity crowdfunding, angel groups, revenue-based financing, government grants, and co-investment schemes such as SEIS/EIS, the EIC Accelerator, Korea's TIPS and Israel's IIA. Geography also matters more than most founders expect. Where you incorporate decides which government money you can tap, and flipping into a Delaware holding company to reach US investors can forfeit home-country incentives worth millions.

## Only about 1 in 200 entrepreneurs uses VC, so test fit before you pitch

You want an investor, and that is reasonable. The first question, though, is what kind of investor your business can actually make money for. The base rates are stark. The Kauffman Foundation estimates that **only 0.5% of entrepreneurs use venture capital**, and that **at least 83% use neither bank loans nor VC** ([Kauffman Capital Access Lab](https://kauffman.org/capital-access-lab/approach)). Older Kauffman research found that even among the fast-growing Inc. 5000, **only 6.5% of surveyed companies had raised VC**. Across about 5,000 firms in the Kauffman Firm Survey, VC was just 4.4% of startup funding. Personal savings, friends and family, and loans made up most of the rest ([Kauffman](https://www.kauffman.org/currents/three-facts-you-probably-didnt-know-about-and-venture-capital-and-entrepreneurship/)). These figures pre-date 2015, but nothing in the 2025-2026 data suggests VC has become more accessible. Seed capital is concentrating in fewer companies, especially in AI ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/18/venture-capital-stalling-seed)).

The test for VC fit is not whether the business is good. It is whether the business could plausibly become worth hundreds of millions to billions of dollars within about a decade. A VC fund needs a few such outcomes to make up for the many companies that fail, so a seed investor asks whether you could return their whole fund. Investors also select on team above product. In the largest survey of institutional VCs, team was rated as more important than technology or business model ([Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/index%2Ephp/faculty-research/publications/how-venture-capitalists-make-decisions)). They also expect a "why now," a large market, and growth that compounds. Benchmarks for sub-$1M ARR SaaS put about **100% year-over-year growth as "good" and 300% as "great"** ([Causo citing High Alpha](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/seed-mrr-benchmarks-2026)). SVB reported that seed companies raising in 2025 had a median revenue base of about $363K, growing 322% a year ([via mean.ceo](https://blog.mean.ceo/?p=5720)). A profitable agency, a restaurant group, a regional services firm or a solid niche SaaS growing 40% a year can be an excellent business and still be a poor VC investment. Selling 7-20% of it to an investor who needs a 10x-plus exit misaligns everyone: the investor pushes for growth the business can't sustain, and the founder ends up with a cap table built for a different company.

The table below maps business types to the investor types that suit them. It is drawn from the funding-instrument research. The fit categories are analytical judgments, while the instruments and figures in it are sourced later in the report.

| Business profile | Best-fit outside capital | Poor fit |
|---|---|---|
| Venture-scale software, AI or deep tech (huge market, 10x+ potential) | Angels/syndicates, then pre-seed SAFE, then top accelerator, then priced seed. Add SBIR/EIC/IIA grants for deep tech | Revenue-based financing before revenue exists |
| Consumer brand with a loyal community | Reg CF / Crowdcube equity crowdfunding, angels, family offices, RBF once sales are steady | Institutional seed VC unless growth is explosive |
| Niche SaaS with steady ARR but moderate growth | Angels, revenue-first "indie" funds, micro-VCs, RBF | Traditional seed VC expecting 3x-a-year growth |
| Local, lifestyle or service business | Friends and family, local angels, community crowdfunding, local grants | VC and accelerators taking 6-10% equity |
| Research- or science-based company | Non-dilutive grants first (SBIR/STTR, EIC, IIA, TIPS), then corporate/strategic investors, then deep-tech VC | Consumer-style crowdfunding |

If you are not VC-fit, the best equity alternatives are angels who invest for 3-5x outcomes and dividends, equity crowdfunding from customers, and strategic or corporate investors who value distribution more than exit multiples. Revenue-based financing is the best near-equity option once revenue is recurring. It is not equity, and it is debt-like, but it is neither a bank loan nor conventional high-interest lending. It is repaid as a share of revenue rather than on a fixed schedule. The research turned up no reliable data on how much capital "revenue-first" funds such as TinySeed or Calm Company Fund deploy. Treat them as a real but small channel.

## Record VC dollars mask a thinning seed market in 2026

The 2025-2026 climate has two sides, and founders need to read both. In aggregate, money is abundant. KPMG reports **US$728.3B of global VC in the first nine months of 2026**, after a first half of $560.4B that was the highest in five years. In Q3 alone, the Americas took $101.2B across 3,947 deals, Asia $39.8B and Europe $28.4B. Europe's total was its eighth straight quarterly increase. KPMG's own headline sums up the shape of the market: "AI megadeals dominate venture capital as investors pour billions into fewer startups" ([KPMG Q3'26](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/media/press-releases/2026/10/ai-megadeals-dominate-venture-capital-as-investors-pour-billions-into-fewer-startups.html); [KPMG H1](https://kpmg.com/ua/en/media/press-releases/2026/08/global-vc-investment-reaches-us-dollar-560-4-billion-at-mid-year.html)). Crunchbase counts Q3 2026 somewhat lower, at about **$159B, of which AI took $102B (64%)**, with a record 27 rounds of $1B or more ([FourWeekMBA on Crunchbase](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-crunchbase-q3-2026-159b-venture-27-billion-dollar-rounds-ai/)). In the US, PitchBook-NVCA data show H1 2026 deal value of $412.7B, with **AI at about 86% and rounds of $100M+ at 87.5% of deployed capital** ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/07/09/pitchbook-us-venture-funding-hits-412-7b-first-half-ai-deals-dominate/)).

The founder-level view is far less generous. US seed funding hit a record $19.4B in 2025, but the classic **$200K-$5M seed round fell from 70% of seed dollars in 2018 to just 26%**, because capital is pooling into oversized AI seeds ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/seed-funding-skewing-larger-ai-competitive-data)). Carta counted more than 11,500 US pre-seed SAFEs and notes in Q2 2026, down from 14,825 a year earlier. The total raised barely moved, so fewer companies are each getting more ([Carta Q2 2026](https://carta.com/data/state-of-pre-seed-q2-2026/)). **46% of seed deals were bridge rounds in Q1 2025**, the highest bridge share Carta had recorded at any stage, and Series A deal counts fell 79% from Q1 2022 to Q1 2025 ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/18/venture-capital-stalling-seed)). The median gap between rounds was **696 days** in Q2 2025 ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/state-of-private-markets-q2-2025/)). Valuations are bimodal. Carta's 95th-percentile seed valuation hit **$200M in Q2 2026, up from $72.2M** a year earlier ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-seed-valuations-95th-percentile-2026/)), while the median stays in the high teens to low $20Ms.

Outside the US the picture is mixed. European startup funding was flat at about **$44B in 2025**, with the UK at $14.4B, Germany $7.4B and France $6.1B ([Maddyness/Atomico](https://www.maddyness.com/2025/11/19/les-startups-europeennes-ont-leve-44-milliards-de-dollars-en-2025/)). Indian tech funding fell 17% to about $10.5B, and **seed was the weakest stage, down 30%** ([The Week/PTI on Tracxn](https://www.theweek.in/wire-updates/business/2025/12/18/dcm91-biz-tech-startups-funding.html)). Southeast Asia hit report-era lows, with H1 2025 at $1.85B and **early stage down about 30%** ([DealStreetAsia](https://media.dealstreetasia.com/stories/southeast-asia-startup-funding-report-h1-2025-summary-455674)). Latin America rose to $4.1B, but seed and angel funding fell 22% to $540M ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/vcs-bullish-latam-startup-funding-rebounds-2025/)). The clear growth stories were the Gulf and Africa. Saudi Arabia raised **$1.72B (+145%)** and led MENA by deal count for the first time ([Arab News on MAGNiTT](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2629071)). Africa rebounded to about **$3.2B (+40%)**, with roughly 83% going to Kenya, South Africa, Egypt and Nigeria ([MEA Tech Watch](https://meatechwatch.com/?p=43437); [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/technology/article/africas-start-up-funding-rebounds-in-2025-after-two-year-slump/)).

Three implications follow. First, raise enough for **24 months or more of runway**, because the next round will take about two years. Second, expect a non-AI pitch to be met with "how does AI fit in?" Many funds have reshaped their mandates around AI ([Qubit Capital](https://qubit.capital/blog/fundraising-for-non-ai-startups)), and startups with AI features raised 83% more than others in 2025 ([HackerNoon](https://sia.hackernoon.com/venture-capital-tilts-toward-ai-as-non-ai-startups-face-funding-squeeze)). Third, consider "seedstrapping": raise one round, then reach revenue with a small team so that a second round becomes optional ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2932811)).

## Angels, accelerators and seed funds form a ladder priced in percentage points

**Angels are the first rung.** Individual checks typically run from **$10K to $100K**. A VentureSouth analysis of more than 1,000 angel transactions found a median of just $10,000 ([AngelBacked](https://www.angelbacked.co/blog/average-angel-investor-check-size)), while other compilations cite $25K-$100K ([Angel Investors Network](https://angelinvestorsnetwork.com/angel-investing/angel-investing-guide-2026)). The figures conflict because the samples differ, and a few wealthy angels writing six-figure checks pull the averages up. Syndicates (AngelList-style special-purpose vehicles led by one angel) let members invest **$1K-$10K each** while showing up as a single line on your cap table ([Angel Investors Network](https://angelinvestorsnetwork.com/capital-raising/angel-investor-minimum-investment-real-2025-numbers); [Metal](https://www.metal.so/collections/angel-syndicate-vs-seed-vc-2025-check-sizes-dilution-guide)). In practice, an angel-only pre-seed of $250K-$1M means collecting somewhere between 5 and 40 checks, or landing one syndicate lead who brings the rest. Local angel groups matter most for founders outside the major hubs and for non-software businesses.

**Accelerators trade equity for money, signal and Demo Day access.** Their terms differ more than founders tend to realise.

| Program | Cash | Equity / instrument | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Y Combinator | $500K | $125K on a post-money SAFE for 7%, plus $375K uncapped MFN SAFE (≈9.5% total at a $15M cap) | Four batches a year. Estimated acceptance about 0.6-1% (not official). Entity must be US, Cayman or Singapore ([YC Deal via thecondia](https://thecondia.com/yc-comibinator-500000-standard-deal/); [wearefounders](https://www.wearefounders.uk/y-combinator-acceptance-rate-2026/); [BetaKit](https://betakit.com/y-combinator-website-no-longer-lists-canada-as-a-country-it-invests-in/)) |
| Techstars (from 2025) | $220K | $200K uncapped MFN SAFE plus $20K convertible equity; 5% common stock | Moved to a YC-like structure in April 2025 ([Techstars](https://www.techstars.com/equity-back-guarantee-the-details)) |
| 500 Global Flagship | $150K | 6% | ([500.co](https://500.co/founders/flagship)) |
| Antler UK (from autumn 2025) | £500K package | £125K for 8.5% plus £85K note; up to £330K pre-committed to the next round | Pre-team / co-founder matching. Terms vary by country ([Antler](https://www.antler.co/press-releases/antler-significantly-increases-investment-for-uks-best-tech-founders)) |
| Entrepreneur First | Stipend, then up to about $250K | Varies; US terms reported at 8% plus MFN (unverified) | Pre-idea, co-founder matching ([superscout](https://superscout.co/investor/entrepreneur-first)) |

A rough way to compare them is the price of 1% of your company at the fixed-equity tranche: YC is about $17.9K per point, 500 Global $25K, and Antler UK about £14.7K. The cash is not what sets them apart. A top-tier program's real value is that investor demand clusters around Demo Day ([YC Blog](https://blog.ycombinator.com/fundraising-advice-for-yc-companies)). A lesser program taking 6-10% for weak signalling is expensive. The YC deal has critics too: some investors argue the uncapped MFN SAFE lets YC crowd angels and micro-VCs out of later rounds ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/?p=273828)). Antler and Entrepreneur First suit solo founders who still need a co-founder. YC, Techstars and 500 suit teams that already exist.

**Pre-seed and seed VC sets the price of the first institutional round.** On Carta, median post-money SAFE caps in 2025 were about **$7.5M for rounds under $250K, $10M for $250K-$1M, and $15M for $1M-$2.5M** ([Carta 2025 review](https://carta.com/sg/en/data/state-of-pre-seed-2025/); [Carta Q2 2025](https://carta.com/data/state-of-pre-seed-q2-2025/)). Carta's Q2 2026 data says caps "keep getting bigger" ([Carta](https://carta.com/data/safe-valuation-caps-q2-2026/)). For priced seed rounds, PitchBook-NVCA medians reported secondhand put US seed pre-money at about **$18.4M in Q1 2026** ([causo](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/h1-2026-state-of-seed-report)). Carta-derived figures show a median of **$4.1M raised at about a $24M post-money, with 18% dilution**, for the six months to July 2026 ([waveup](https://waveup.com/blog/seed-round-benchmarks/)). These 2026 numbers come from secondary relays of Carta and PitchBook, and Carta's cap tables over-represent San Francisco and AI companies. Founders outside the Bay Area or NYC, and outside AI, should anchor below these medians. Micro-VCs and rolling funds, typically funds under $50-100M writing $100K-$1M first checks, are the usual leads at pre-seed. Corporate VCs are surprisingly active early: US corporate VCs did about **35% of their deals at seed** from 2022 to 2025 ([Deloitte/PitchBook](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-zone3/ca/en/docs/services/program/state-of-corporate-venture-2026-aoda-en.pdf)). They bring distribution, but can also bring signalling risk and slower decisions.

## SAFEs dominate early rounds, and stacking them quietly costs founders control

The post-money SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is the default early-stage instrument. SAFEs were a **record 90% of Carta pre-seed rounds in Q1 2025**. Convertible notes made up the remaining 10%, at a median 7% interest rate ([Carta Q1 2025](https://carta.com/data/state-of-pre-seed-q1-2025/)). Most SAFEs carry a valuation cap and no discount. One summary of Carta data finds **61% are cap-only**, and where a discount exists it is 20% in most cases ([Kruze Consulting](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/preseed-funding/)). SAFEs let you close investors one at a time as each commits, without waiting to fill a round, and that rolling close is how founders build momentum ([Lunaray on YC guidance](https://lunaray.substack.com/p/fundraising-with-safes)).

The mechanics, taken from the standard YC documents, are simple and unforgiving. Under a post-money SAFE, investor ownership equals the amount invested divided by the post-money cap: **$1M on a $10M cap buys 10%**. Because the cap already includes all SAFE money, the founders absorb all the dilution from every additional SAFE. A convertible note is technically debt, with interest and a maturity date. If no priced round arrives before maturity, you face repayment or renegotiation. That is why founders prefer SAFEs, although some investors and non-US jurisdictions still use notes. A priced round, usually the seed or Series A, sets a valuation and issues preferred stock under a term sheet. The term sheet covers liquidation preference (the norm is 1x non-participating), board seats, pro rata rights and protective provisions. It costs more in legal fees and takes longer to close.

The real costs show up at conversion. Carta's 2025 data puts median dilution per round, from seed through Series C, at about **16%**, and **median founding-team ownership at 55.1% after seed, 36.6% after Series A and 23.5% after Series B**. Only about one founding team in five still holds a majority after Series A ([Carta 2025 review](https://carta.com/data/state-of-private-markets-q4-2025-full-report/); [Carta](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-founders-rarely-majority-after-series-a/)). Option-pool top-ups and SAFE conversions that land at the priced round commonly make real dilution **5-10 points worse than the headline figure** ([Causo](https://hub.causo.ai/guides/founder-dilution-benchmarks-across-rounds-2026)). YC's rule of thumb is to sell about 20% at seed, or 25% for a larger round ([YC Blog](https://blog.ycombinator.com/fundraising-advice-for-yc-companies)). So model your cap table before you sign each SAFE, not after. The retrieved sources did not cover predatory terms, so the following is general guidance rather than sourced data. Be wary of full-ratchet anti-dilution, participating preferred stock or liquidation preferences above 1x, redemption rights, and very low caps stacked across many SAFEs.

Outside the US the instruments change. UK angel rounds are built around SEIS/EIS-compliant shares or advance subscription agreements, and other jurisdictions use their own variants. The research found no comparative data on these, so check local norms with a lawyer.

## Crowdfunding, grants and corporate money cover what VC won't

**Equity crowdfunding** is the most direct way to get "investors" for a business that is not VC-fit. In the US, Reg CF allows raises of **up to $5M per 12 months**, and Reg A+ Tier 2 allows up to $75M ([Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/?p=188205); [StartEngine](https://www.startengine.com/insights/private-market-education/investing-in-private-companies/reg-a-vs-reg-d-vs-reg-cf)). The realistic numbers are smaller. Kingscrowd counted $378.3M raised through Reg CF in 2025 across 1,189 closings. **67.4% of those hit their target, with a median successful raise of about $194K** and an average of $572K ([Angel Investors Network on Kingscrowd](https://angelinvestorsnetwork.com/startups/reg-cf-vs-reg-a-plus-2025-scorecard-924-million-crowdfunding-data)). Wefunder led with about $109M, ahead of StartEngine at about $89M. In the UK, Crowdcube remains independent, and Seedrs became Republic Europe after Republic acquired it ([Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/2024/07/227383-seedrs-to-rebrand-as-republic-europe/)). Through its EU-authorised entity, Crowdcube lets a company raise **up to €5M from EU investors plus the equivalent of €8M from UK investors** each year ([Crowdsourcing Week](https://crowdsourcingweek.com/?p=12140)). Crowdfunding turns customers into evangelists, and pooling investors through a crowd SAFE or SPV keeps the cap table tidy. In return you take on public financial disclosure and ongoing annual reporting. It suits consumer brands, community-anchored businesses and local ventures. It cannot replace a $3-4M institutional seed.

**Non-dilutive grants** are the most underused money in deep tech. US SBIR/STTR lapsed in October 2025 and was **reauthorized on April 13, 2026 through September 30, 2031** ([Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/sbirsttr-programs-reauthorized-after-six-month-lapse)). NSF Phase I pays up to $305K and Phase II up to $1.25M. NIH Phase I pays up to about $295K. The new law adds a Phase II "strategic breakthrough" award of up to $30M ([casrai](https://www.casrai.org/news/nsf-sbir-sttr-fy26-relaunch)). A combined Phase I and II can rival a seed round with zero dilution. The restart has been uneven, though: Department of Defense solicitations resumed first ([OpenGrants](https://opengrants.io/sbir-grants-2026-what-changed-whats-next/)). The program requires US ownership and now applies stricter foreign-risk screening.

**Revenue-based financing** sits at the edge of what you asked for. It is not equity, but it is non-dilutive and not a bank loan. Providers typically cap total repayment at **1.2-2.0x the amount advanced**, collected as 2-8% of monthly revenue. That works out to effective annual costs of about **12% if repaid over four years and over 25% if repaid in under two** ([Angel Investors Network](https://angelinvestorsnetwork.com/alternative-investments/revenue-based-financing-accredited-investors-explained-2026)). E-commerce-focused providers such as Clearco can sweep up to about 30% of weekly sales, at an effective cost of roughly 14-40% ([eightx](https://eightx.co/blog/compare/reviews/clearco-for-ecommerce-review)). Use it only once revenue is predictable, and avoid it if your margins are thin.

## Geography decides which government will co-invest beside you

Governments increasingly act as co-investors and limited partners rather than handing out grants directly. The pattern is consistent across regions: the state matches private money once a qualified private investor commits. So the order of moves matters. Win a private lead, then unlock the public match.

| Gulf | Hub71 offers up to **AED 750K** in cash and in-kind support if a founder relocates ([MEA Tech Watch](https://meatechwatch.com/2025/11/03/abu-dhabi-scales-up-startup-innovation-through-enhanced-hub71-incentive-program/)); Saudi Venture Capital Company acts as LP and co-investor ([GEN Atlas](https://www.genglobal.org/atlas/saudi-venture-capital-company-svc)); QIA's $1B fund-of-funds says Doha's gap is Series A-C, not seed ([Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/02/26/2025/qatar-investment-authority-lures-vc-firms-to-doha-with-1b-fund)) | Budget for a real local entity and team; sovereign-backed funds reward local job creation |
| Israel | IIA incubators fund up to 60% at pre-seed (up to NIS 1.5M); the IIA Seed Program funds **40% of a VC-led seed, up to NIS 3.5M** ([IIA](https://innovationisrael.org.il/en/?p=4102)) | Pair an IIA grant with a term sheet from a qualified VC; check IP-transfer restrictions |
| Africa | Equity-free Google for Startups Accelerator: Africa (up to $350K in cloud credits) ([Google](https://startup.google.com/accelerator/africa)); development-finance-backed seed programs such as Digital Africa and Flourish's Madica ($200K checks) ([Launch Base Africa](https://launchbaseafrica.com/2025/08/20/the-most-active-investors-in-african-tech-startups-h1-2025/)) | Outside the Big Four countries, rely on development-finance programs and equity-free accelerators |

The cross-border decision deserves attention early. US accelerators and VCs generally require a US parent company. A "Delaware flip," in which a new US parent owns your existing company, costs an estimated **$45K-$150K+**, versus a few thousand dollars to set up a simple US subsidiary ([We Are Founders](https://www.wearefounders.uk/why-y-combinator-just-narrowed-the-map-for-canadian-founders/)). For Canadian and UK founders, a flip can forfeit SR&ED refunds or SEIS/EIS reliefs, and those may be worth more than the US capital it unlocks. Many founders delay the flip until a US investor is ready to lead.

## Warm intros and a compressed process win most rounds

How deals get sourced explains most of what works. In a survey of 885 institutional VCs, **over 30% of deals came from the investors' professional networks, 20% from referrals by other investors, 8% from portfolio companies, and only 10% inbound from founders**. The median firm looked at about 100 companies for every one it funded ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/dec16/w22587.html)). The data is from 2016, but the network effect it describes has not disappeared. The strongest intros come from founders the investor has already backed and from angels already on your cap table. Generic "connectors" are weaker. Cold outreach still produces about 10% of deals, but at low hit rates. Vendor data suggests 1-5% of cold emails get a reply, and about one meeting per 50-100 targeted contacts ([Metal](https://www.metal.so/collections/warm-intros-vs-cold-email-2025-conversion-data-referral-playbook); [Founders Capital](https://founderscapital8.substack.com/p/cold-emails-to-vcs-have-a-95-non)). Investors accept about **25% of double-opt-in intro requests** ([Metal](https://www.metal.so/data-desk/intro-response-benchmark)), so give each connector a short blurb they can forward unchanged. One contrarian data point: an analysis of cold outreach to 187 VC firms suggested cold-sourced deals perform no worse, though causation is unproven ([AngelBacked](https://www.angelbacked.co/blog/cold-email-vs-warm-intro-vc-returns-research)). Free investor-search tools such as Signal by NFX and OpenVC, plus Crunchbase deal histories, help build the target list. The research did not measure how well these tools convert.

The pitch itself is read fast. DocSend's research shows investors spend **under three minutes per deck**. Decks that raised drew over four minutes of reading, against 1.5 minutes for decks that did not ([Dropbox DocSend](https://www.dropbox.com/resources/docsend-pitch-deck-research)). The standard 10-12 slides cover problem, solution, why now, market, product, traction, business model, competition, team and the ask ([Slidebean Sequoia template](https://slidebean.com/templates/sequoia-pitch-deck-template)). Put traction and team early and make them easy to scan. For AI companies, investors now test cohort retention and how many pilots convert to production, because AI "ARR" often contains one-off revenue ([CRV](https://www.crv.com/content/key-performance-indicators)).

Run the process as a sprint, not a trickle. An older DocSend study found startups contacted an average of **58 investors, held 40 meetings and took 12.5 weeks** to close. The number of investors contacted did not correlate with the amount raised ([PYMNTS](https://pymnts.com/?p=145925)). Current benchmarks put first-to-second-meeting conversion at **about 35-37%**, reaching 75-85% only for breakout traction ([Metal](https://www.metal.so/data-desk/1st-to-2nd-meeting-conversion)). If two-thirds of first meetings go nowhere, that is normal. YC advises its companies to concentrate meetings into one window and close the first reasonable checks early rather than waiting to fill the round ([YC Blog](https://blog.ycombinator.com/fundraising-advice-for-yc-companies)).

Finally, screen your investors as hard as they screen you. **Legitimate investors never charge founders a fee.** Upfront "due diligence," "compliance," "legal" or "escrow" fees are the signature of advance-fee fraud ([SEC Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alert-be-lookout-advance-fee-fraud)). Self-styled syndicates charging **$5,000-$20,000 to pitch** rarely deliver access ([Rare Founders](https://rarefounders-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/10-fundraising-scams-every-startup-founder-should-know-2025)). Fake "analysts" sometimes collect financial models and customer lists and then vanish ([Crunchbase](https://about.crunchbase.com/blog/startup-scams-and-scam-adjacent-activity)). Check the investor's deal history and call founders they have backed.

## A step-by-step playbook from fit test to wired funds

The sequence below pulls the evidence above into one path. The timings are planning estimates based on the benchmarks cited, not guarantees.

| Step | What to do | Why (evidence anchor) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Fit test (week 0) | Decide honestly whether the business can plausibly reach a $500M+ outcome with 100%+ annual growth. If not, follow the crowdfunding, angel group, RBF and grant route in the fit table | Only about 0.5% of entrepreneurs use VC; seed growth bars are 100-300% a year |
| 2. Choose your jurisdiction and claim free money first | Apply for non-dilutive and co-investment programs: SBIR, EIC, IIA, TIPS, SR&ED, Start-Up Chile. In the UK, get SEIS/EIS advance assurance. Delay a Delaware flip until a US lead requires it | Grants such as SBIR Phase I+II (about $1.5M) rival a seed round with zero dilution; a flip can cost $45K-$150K+ and forfeit local incentives |
| 3. Get proof (months 1-3) | Reach whatever traction your stage demands: users, pilots, revenue, retention cohorts. Build a 10-12 slide deck and a basic data room (incorporation documents, cap table, IP assignments, financial model; general practice) | Investors read decks in under 3 minutes; team and traction slides decide the read |
| 4. Model the round | Set a target raise for 24+ months of runway, plan to sell about 15-20%, and choose your instrument (post-money SAFE for pre-seed, priced round for a $3M+ seed). Model every SAFE and the option pool together | About 2-year gaps between rounds; real dilution often runs 5-10 points above headline |
| 5. Consider an accelerator | Apply to YC, Techstars or 500 if you are a team; Antler or EF if you need a co-founder. Skip programs whose signal doesn't justify 6-10% | Demo Day concentrates investor demand; a weak program is expensive equity |
| 6. Build the target list | 100-150 investors, tiered by thesis fit and check size, with likely leads first: micro-VCs, seed funds, angel syndicates, relevant corporate VCs, government-matched funds. Map a warm path to each | 20% of deals come via other investors and 8% via portfolio founders |
| 7. Collect intros, then sprint (weeks 1-4) | Send forwardable blurbs to connectors and stack first meetings into a 2-4 week window. Use cold email only with a sharp hook | About 25% of intro requests are accepted; cold email replies run about 1-5% |
| 8. Convert and close on a rolling basis | Expect about a 35% first-to-second meeting rate. Sign SAFEs as checks commit to build momentum; find a lead or anchor to set terms | Benchmarks: about 40 meetings and about 12 weeks per round |
| 9. Screen and protect | Never pay to pitch or pay "fees." Run reference calls with portfolio founders. Get a lawyer to review anything beyond standard SAFE or 1x non-participating terms | Advance-fee fraud and pay-to-pitch schemes are common |
| 10. Fall back intelligently | If VC says no after a full process, pivot to crowdfunding (median successful Reg CF raise about $194K), angels, RBF once revenue is steady, or "seedstrapping" to profitability | Most durable companies are funded outside VC |

## Conclusion

The equity market of 2026 is not short of capital. It is short of capital for the median company. That changes what a founder should optimise. Valuation matters less than runway, because rounds are about two years apart and seed deal counts are falling. Investor count matters less than investor fit, because a single lead or a government match moves a round further than fifty cold emails. Headline geography matters less than incorporation geography, because whether you can access SR&ED, SEIS/EIS, TIPS, IIA or Startup SG Equity depends on where your company sits. That is also why a hasty Delaware flip can be one of the costliest early mistakes.

The more counterintuitive lesson is that state money now often comes before VC money. Across Israel, Korea, Singapore, the EU and the UK, governments price risk by matching private investors rather than replacing them. A founder who stacks a grant or co-investment on a modest angel round can often reach the next milestone with less dilution than a pure-VC path would cost. If an honest fit test says your business will never be a venture outlier, that is not a dead end. It means the right investors are your customers, local angels and strategic partners, and the right instruments are crowdfunding, revenue-based financing and grants. The research is weaker in places: many 2026 seed medians come through secondary relays, warm-intro conversion data comes from vendors, and there is little non-US data on seed valuations or instruments. Treat the numbers here as planning ranges, not quotes.
